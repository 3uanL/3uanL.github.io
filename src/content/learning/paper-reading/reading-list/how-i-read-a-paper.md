Reading a paper used to mean starting at the abstract and pushing downhill until my attention ran
out somewhere in Section III. What changed it was writing down, before the first equation, three
things: *what problem the authors claim to solve*, *what they assume*, and *what I would have tried
myself*. Everything after that is checking those three against the paper.

## The routine

1. **Title, abstract, figures, conclusion** — five minutes. If the figures do not make sense, the
   paper is not about numbers, it is about a picture, and I need to find that picture first.
2. **Rewrite the problem statement in my own words** — one paragraph, no symbols. If I cannot do
   this without copying sentences, I have not understood what is being optimized.
3. **Find the single equation that carries the paper** — usually the objective or the update rule.
   Everything else is bookkeeping around it.
4. **Re-derive one step by hand** — pick the step the authors wave through. If it survives my
   handwriting, I trust the rest of the derivation more.
5. **Write the note** — what I would keep, what I would not, and one experiment I would run.

## What goes in a note here

Each paper note ends with the same three lines, because they are the only ones I re-read later:

- **Claim**: what the paper says it achieves.
- **Trick**: the one idea that makes it work.
- **Doubt**: the thing I still do not believe.

The doubt line is the useful one. A paper that leaves me with no doubt probably did not teach me
anything — it just agreed with what I already thought.
